# Authenticated Session Watermarks for Agent-to-Agent Provenance

## Abstract

This study evaluates an authenticated, session-derived text watermark as an early provenance signal for agent-to-agent communication. A keyed XRF frame was embedded invisibly using Unicode variation selectors and protected with repetition coding. Six long-form Gemini outputs were tested under 6,480 randomized perturbation trials, three live model-mediated handoffs, and one mixed-lineage scenario. Clean exact recovery and visible-text preservation were both 100%. Fivefold repetition retained 100% recovery under 10% selector corruption and 10–20% selector loss, but fell to 5.8% under 10% word deletion. All live Gemini handoffs stripped the origin mark despite preserving the visible output in these runs. A layered artifact retained and correctly classified three session marks, allowing two authorized contributors to be reviewed while a known-malicious contributor alone triggered blocking. The results support authenticated watermarks as a useful hop-local provenance feature, but reject them as a standalone sandbox or end-to-end security mechanism.

## Research question

Can a keyed text watermark identify session lineage after long outputs are partially altered, while preserving readable content and avoiding the assumption that every cross-session contribution is malicious?

## Method

The frame contains a rotating `XRF` family prefix, a version, an authenticated session tag derived with HMAC, and CRC integrity. One encoded byte is attached to each eligible word through an invisible variation selector. Repetition factors 1, 3, and 5 were evaluated. The decoder accepts only complete, authenticated frames linked to a session registry.

The corpus comprises six documents generated by `gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, with target lengths of 250, 500, and 1,000 words. For every document, perturbation type, severity, and redundancy, 20 seeded trials were executed. Perturbations independently removed carrier symbols, corrupted carrier symbols, or deleted visible words. Three representative documents were also sent through a second Gemini call before adding an editor-session watermark. Finally, sections bearing three different session marks were assembled into one artifact and evaluated against authorized and malicious registry states.

## Results

The prototype achieved perfect recovery without perturbation and did not alter visible text. Fivefold repetition was strongest for symbol-local errors: exact recovery was 100% at 10% selector corruption, 85% at 20% corruption, 100% at 20% selector loss, and 95% at 35% selector loss. Synchronization errors were substantially more damaging. At 5% word deletion, recovery was 38.3%, 35%, and 23.3% for r1, r3, and r5 respectively; at 10%, it was 29.2%, 5%, and 5.8%.

Results were identical across target document lengths because the implementation places a fixed-length frame near the start instead of distributing information through available capacity. This demonstrates that longer input alone does not improve robustness.

In all three live handoffs, the origin mark disappeared and the newly applied editor mark decoded correctly. The visible similarity ratio was 1.0, indicating normalization of invisible Unicode rather than meaningful visible rewriting in those calls. The mixed artifact yielded three valid identities. The two authorized sessions were labeled for cross-session review, whereas only the registry-listed malicious session produced a block decision.

## Discussion

The watermark answers “does this input contain an authenticated mark issued by our system?” It cannot reliably answer “is this content malicious?” or “did no unmarked agent touch it?” A valid watermark is positive evidence of one lineage event; absence is inconclusive; and a malicious actor with access to marked text can strip the current carrier.

The next design should encode multiple independently recoverable shards across the document, include synchronization markers or content-derived anchors, and use erasure-oriented coding. A benchmark should separate accidental transformations from adaptive attacks and report false positives on a large unwatermarked corpus. Policy should reason over a provenance graph: identity, authorization, route, tool scope, timestamps, and transformation history. Blocking should require a valid match to a revoked or malicious principal, or corroborating behavioral evidence.

## Threats to validity

The corpus and live handoff sample are small, English-only, and limited to one model. Synthetic errors do not approximate every tokenizer, editor, messaging platform, or adversarial rewrite. The invisible carrier is conspicuous to a Unicode-aware adversary. The study does not yet quantify false positives over natural web text, key compromise, collusion, replay, or cross-tenant isolation. Production deployment would require privacy review because session-linked provenance can become a tracking mechanism.

## Conclusion

Authenticated session watermarks are promising as low-latency provenance telemetry between cooperating gateways. They can preserve readable output, support multi-contributor attribution, and help a registry distinguish benign from malicious lineage. The present carrier is robust to local symbol damage but brittle to structural edits and normalization. It should complement—never replace—sandboxing, least-privilege tool authorization, signed envelopes, and runtime behavior monitoring.
